How to Choose a Firefighter Radio Strap

RADIO STRAPS · 8 MIN READ

A radio strap is a simple thing. A length of leather, some hardware, and a holder for your portable. That simplicity is exactly why the differences between a good one and a bad one are easy to miss until you're wearing it.

Here's what actually matters, roughly in the order it'll matter to you.

1. Start with where the radio needs to sit

Before leather, before color, before any of it: decide where you want the radio. That single choice drives your length, and length is the thing people get wrong.

Most people want the radio riding at or just above the hip, on the opposite side from the shoulder the strap crosses. High enough that your coat doesn't swallow it. Low enough that the mic cord isn't fighting your collar. Sit down in it, reach across, key the mic. If you have to hunt for it, it's in the wrong place.

Once you know where it sits, measure. Two methods, both take about a minute, both on the how to measure page.

Measure before you shop, not after. Every strap worth buying is made to order, which means the length gets cut once. Getting it wrong costs you weeks and usually a fee.

2. Leather weight

Leather thickness is measured in ounces. One ounce is about 1/64 of an inch, so 10–12 oz leather runs roughly 5/32" to 3/16" thick.

That number tells you most of what you need to know. Thin leather — anything you can fold easily in your hand — will roll up on itself under the weight of a portable and stay rolled. Once a strap takes a permanent twist, it never really comes back. Heavy leather holds its shape, which is the entire job.

Heavy leather is also stiff out of the box, and people mistake that for a defect. It isn't. A strap that feels broken in on day one is a strap that will feel dead in a year. Give it a few shifts.

We cut at 10–12 oz. If you're comparing elsewhere, 8 oz is about the floor for a strap carrying a radio full time.

3. Width

Radio straps generally land between 1.25" and 2" wide.

  • Narrow — less bulk, but the load concentrates on a smaller patch of your shoulder. You notice it on long calls.
  • Wide — spreads the weight, but gets bulky under a coat and can crowd your SCBA straps.

We cut at 1.5", which is the middle of that range for a reason: it carries the weight without adding bulk where the strap crosses your shoulder.

4. What holds it together

This is the part people skip, and it's the part that fails. The leather is rarely what gives out first — the hardware is.

  • Snaps — fast to adjust, and heavy-duty ones take a real beating. Cheap ones work loose.
  • Chicago screws — a threaded post and cap. You tighten it once and it stays. Slower to adjust, harder to lose.
  • Stitching — not adjustable at all, but nothing to work loose. Usually combined with one of the above.

Whatever the strap uses, the question is the same: what happens to this after a year of being yanked, sat on, and soaked? Ask before you buy. A shop that builds its own gear will have an answer.

5. The holster and the sway strap

A strap with no radio holder is half a purchase. Holders bought separately rarely match the leather or the hardware, and the fit is a coin flip.

Two things to confirm before you order:

  • The holder fits your portable. Most holders are built around common portable radios. If you carry something unusual, say so before the leather gets cut.
  • An anti-sway strap is included. Without one, the radio swings out from your body every time you move. It's a small piece of leather that makes a large difference, and it's the first thing cut from a cheap package.

Both of ours ship with a black leather radio holder and an anti-sway strap. That's the baseline, not the upgrade.

6. Fixed or adjustable

A fixed-length strap is cut to one length and stays there. An adjustable strap gives you a range to work inside, which covers seasonal bulk — a station uniform in July versus a coat in January is a real difference in how much strap you need.

Both of ours are adjustable, and we think that's the right default for most people. The full trade-off is its own guide: fixed length vs adjustable radio straps.

7. Personalization

Name, unit number, department insignia, a memorial. This is where a radio strap stops being equipment and starts being yours.

Worth knowing before you compare prices: hand-stamped and painted lettering is slower and more expensive than printing or laser work, and it holds up differently. Stamping presses the letter into the leather — it's still there when the paint wears. Surface-printed lettering wears off.

If you're ordering for a promotion, a retirement, or a memorial, say so in the message field. Those builds usually need a conversation before anything gets cut.

8. Lead time is a feature

Anything genuinely made to order takes weeks, not days. Ours is six weeks on radio straps. A shop promising a custom leather strap in three days is either sitting on pre-made stock or cutting a corner somewhere you can't see yet.

Plan around it. If it's a retirement gift, order two months out.

9. Know what it isn't

Radio straps are accessory items. They are not PPE and they are not NFPA certified — ours included. Check your department's policy on aftermarket accessories before you wear one on a call. Read our use disclaimer.

The short version

  • Decide where the radio sits, then measure. Twice.
  • 8 oz leather minimum. Heavier holds its shape longer.
  • 1.5" is a safe width.
  • Ask what the hardware is and what happens to it in a year.
  • Holster and anti-sway strap should be included, not extra.
  • Adjustable covers seasonal bulk. Fixed is simpler.
  • Stamped lettering outlasts printed lettering.
  • Expect weeks, not days.
